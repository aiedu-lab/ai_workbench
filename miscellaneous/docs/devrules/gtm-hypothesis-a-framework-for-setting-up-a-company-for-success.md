# Introduction {#introduction}

Building a successful company is hard. Building unicorns is even harder. Often it is a roll of dice whether the company will be successful or not. Unfortunately, a lot of money, time, and effort is wasted before it becomes clear whether a company is going to be successful. Course corrections late in the game are also very hard and expensive, and not many companies are able to survive them.

This document offers a framework that can be used to predict the future success of a company much earlier \- during the inception of the company. That makes course corrections possible much earlier, thereby significantly improving the odds of building a successful company \- with a higher probability of becoming a unicorn.

The essence of the framework is a GTM Hypothesis that forces the founding team to build the company’s blueprint and evaluate it objectively. 

# GTM Hypothesis {#gtm-hypothesis}

The GTM Hypothesis is a document that consists of the following four sections:

1. Elevator pitch  
2. Description of the Minimum Lovable Product (MLP)  
3. Why would the company succeed   
4. Why would the company NOT succeed and the corresponding rebuttals by the founding team

## Elevator pitch {#elevator-pitch}

The Elevator pitch is a crisp description of what the company does, and why customers should be interested in it. It is quite literally what needs to be said to a prospective customer who one runs into in an elevator. If you can’t interest the customer during that elevator ride, you have the wrong Elevator pitch.

A good Elevator pitch should bring out the following crisply:

1. Problem: What is the problem the company is addressing, and why is that a real pain for the customer.  
2. Solution: The company’s solution to the above problem, and why it is a must-have, not just a good-to-have. And the bigger vision of the company on how the solution would evolve over time.  
3. Differentiation: How the company’s solution differentiated from other competitors.

## Description of the Minimum Lovable Product (MLP) {#description-of-the-minimum-lovable-product-(mlp)}

The initial success of a company is determined by the first product version it launches. Based on that, investors will provide more funding to the company, and it’ll be able to grow by attracting good people. That’s why the GTM Hypothesis should focus on that first version of the product. It needs to be Minimum possible that customers would find Lovable and would pay for. That’s why we call it the Minimum Lovable Product.

The various features of the MLP should be captured in the second section of the GTM Hypothesis. It essentially feeds into a Product Requirements Document later on.

Crisply capturing the MLP helps build the subsequent sections on why the company would succeed or NOT succeed. Often the founding team makes the mistake of basing their assumptions on a version of the product that comes much later. Forcing the MLP to be written down would help remove that mistake.

## Why would the company succeed {#why-would-the-company-succeed}

This section should capture all the reasons why the company would succeed. E.g., it should bring out what the competitive differentiators are in the MLP, and how the MLP solves an immediate pain for the customer. 

Unless a compelling case can be made in this section on why the company would succeed, in real life, the founding team would just find out the hard way after multiple years that the company isn’t succeeding. 

## Why would the company NOT succeed and the corresponding rebuttals by the founding team {#why-would-the-company-not-succeed-and-the-corresponding-rebuttals-by-the-founding-team}

The founding team should either put itself in the shoes of naysayers, or else talk to enough external people (especially prospective customers) so as to surface naysayer arguments on why the company won’t succeed. Each of these arguments should be written down in this section, and a rebuttal should be provided against that by the founding team. 

The rebuttal should not just be emotional (e.g., ‘We are confident this won’t be a problem’). A litmus test is that an objective person reading the naysayer argument and the rebuttal can become convinced that the rebuttal has legs.

# Checklist {#checklist}

Here’s a checklist of things to consider while building the last two sections of the GTM Hypothesis. 

1. The TAM should be at least a few billion dollars. Investors don’t like investing in small markets.  
2. The neighborhood in which the company operates shouldn’t be a graveyard of lots of dead companies.   
3. It should be possible to build the MLP well before the Series A funding runs out. Otherwise subsequent funding may not come.  
4. There should be some alternative pivot points that the company can sell into. This helps recover from blind-spots in the GTM Hypothesis later on \- which might manifest itself as lack-luster reception of the product by customers.  
5. There should be a significant moat between the company and its competition. And it should be possible to maintain this moat over time.. Some of the ways this can be done are as follows:  
   * The barrier for other competitors to build a similar product could be high.  
   * The company’s vision and roadmap beyond the initial MLP can be such that it maintains the moat. By the time the competitors catch up to what the company has on the truck (i.e., what they’re selling currently), the company has moved ahead and added more to the truck.  
   * There may be network effects that prevent customers from easily switching to a competitor. E.g., an auction site, or a social media platform.  
6. The company should be aligned with the industry trends.  
7. The MLP should address some immediate pain. It shouldn’t just be a good-to-have.  
8. The MLP should make for a good insertion point in the customer’s environment \- not an additional headache.  
9. It should take no more than 5-7 yrs after launching the MLP to reach a level of customer adoption that’ll make the company a unicorn.  
10. If the product requires high touch, it should be possible to command a high price for it. Conversely, if a high price cannot be demanded for the product per customer, then it should be a low touch product and it should be possible to sell it quickly to a lot of customers.

A detailed description of the above is also available at [https://rajeshsetty.com/2017/06/29/mohit-aron-evaluating-ideas/](https://rajeshsetty.com/2017/06/29/mohit-aron-evaluating-ideas/).

# Verification {#verification}

It is important to have several people \- especially outside the founding team \- look at the hypothesis and provide an objective assessment. The founding team often is biased to drink its own kool-aid \- so might find it hard to be objective in its assessment.

To make for a more data-driven assessment, one may assign a number of attributes to each section and have an assessor rate against those attributes. For example, to assess the Elevator Pitch, one might use the following attributes: (1) Crispness, (2) Solves an immediate pain, (3) Presents a bigger vision, (4) Differentiated. An assessor can be asked to rate each attribute using 1=Poor, 2=Fair, 3=Good, 4=Excellent. These attributes may vary based on the kind of company one is building.

If the consensus is that the GTM Hypothesis is tight, then the company essentially has a solid blueprint to start with and there’s a good chance it’ll be successful \- perhaps even enough to be a unicorn one day.

# Keeping it fresh {#keeping-it-fresh}

The GTM Hypothesis shouldn’t be forgotten after the inception of the company. It should be continuously consulted and updated as more information is discovered. E.g., more naysayer arguments may come up well after the initial GTM Hypothesis was built. These should be incorporated in the GTM Hypothesis and rebuttals written against them.

# Use in already successful companies {#use-in-already-successful-companies}

While this document is written primarily for a new company, it doesn’t mean the concept of a GTM Hypothesis cannot be used by an already successful company. Whenever new products are introduced in such companies, building a GTM Hypothesis for that product will ensure that product will have a much higher chance of success. Way too many new products introduced by companies don’t live up to their expectations otherwise.
