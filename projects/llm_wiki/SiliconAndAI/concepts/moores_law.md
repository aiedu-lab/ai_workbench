# Moore's Law

The empirical observation that the number of transistors in an
[[integrated circuit]] doubles approximately every two years,
with minimal increase in cost. Named after [[Gordon Moore]],
co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and Intel.

## Origin
Articulated by [[Gordon Moore]] in a 1965 article "Cramming
More Components Onto Integrated Circuits" in *Electronics*
magazine. He initially predicted doubling every year; in 1975
he revised the forecast to every two years.

## Dennard Scaling Corollary
[[Robert Dennard]] showed that as transistors shrank, power
density stayed constant — so performance doubled at no extra
power cost. [[Dennard Scaling]] ended around 2005–2010 due to
current leakage at nanoscale. This ended the era of free
single-core performance gains.

## Impact on AI
Increasing compute enabled by Moore's Law was the primary driver
of AI breakthroughs:
- [[Deep Blue]]'s 1997 chess victory used a machine 10 million
  times faster than the 1951 Ferranti Mark 1 — all due to
  transistor scaling.
- The [[Deep Learning]] breakthrough ([[AlexNet]], 2012) required
  GPU compute only possible through decades of Moore's Law
  scaling.
- Modern [[Large Language Models]] (GPT-4, Claude) require
  chips with tens of billions of transistors.

## Slowing
Semiconductor advancement slowed industry-wide since ~2010.
NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang declared Moore's Law dead in 2022;
Intel's Pat Gelsinger disagreed. Doubling time has stretched
from 2 years toward 3+ years in the 2020s.
